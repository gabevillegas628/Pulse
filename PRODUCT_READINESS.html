Pulse · Engineering & commercial readiness

What it would take to run Pulse for someone else

Written ahead of the November talk, so there is a real answer when somebody says they'd like to use it. Three thresholds, what stands between here and each one, and what it actually costs to run.

Updated 2 September 2026. Gate A is closed — four items fixed and a fifth withdrawn — and the first Gate B item with it: ownership is now enforced in one place rather than fifty. There is also a scratch database now, cloned from prod, which is what finally let the integration suites run.

Updated 3 September 2026. The admin role is closed — the second Gate B item: a system view, account creation, transfer, and deactivation, built without widening what ownership means. Deleting a professor is now refused by the database while their classes remain.

Updated 4 September 2026. Self-service password reset is closed — the third Gate B item. Brevo is wired in behind a provider-agnostic seam, the sending domain is authenticated, and a student who has forgotten their password no longer needs their professor to be in the room.

Where it stands

A well-built single-tenant app wearing a multi-tenant shape

The code is in better condition than most things at this stage. The hard parts — the arming threshold on the close countdown, monotonic deadlines, token renewal on activity, warming clocks from Response.submittedAt after a restart — are not just implemented but reasoned about in the comments. That is the part that is genuinely difficult to buy.

What is missing is everything that assumes there is exactly one professor, one machine, and one person who cares when it breaks. None of it is deep, but it is broad, and it does not surface until a second person shows up. The list below is that list.

Testing is further along than it looks from the outside: there are 246 assertions in backend/scripts/ across six suites, including a real unit suite for the clock driven on injected timestamps. All six pass. The clock half runs on every push; the other five have been run by hand against a clone of prod and want only a Postgres service in CI to join it — plus a decision about an API key, since one of them spends real tokens.

The framing

Three thresholds, not one

"Could I use it?" means three very different things depending on who is asking, and they need almost disjoint work. Getting this distinction straight is most of the value of having thought about it in advance — it converts a vague, alarming question into a specific, answerable one.

Gate A

Survive this semester

One course, you operating it, you on the hook. The bar is that a lecture never fails in a way you can't recover from in the room.

Closed. Four items fixed, and a fifth that turned out not to be one.

Gate B

A second instructor at Rutgers

Someone you know, same institution, no contract. The bar shifts from "it works" to "it is isolated, recoverable, and doesn't need you in the room."

Weeks of work. The admin role and password reset are in; spend caps and a tested backup are the bulk of what is left.

Gate C

Another institution

People you've never met, whose IT department has opinions. The bar is procurement, not engineering.

Months, and mostly not code. SSO, LTI, accessibility, a security questionnaire, an entity.

Closed

What has actually been closed

All of Gate A — four items, plus one that turned out not to be an item at all — and the first three Gate B items: ownership in one place, the admin role that consolidation was priced for, and self-service password reset.

No self-service password resetservices/password-reset.service.ts · services/email.service.ts Built Closed, and the mail provider it was waiting on is in with it. A student asks for a link with their NetID or email and gets one at the address on their account. The link carries 32 bytes from the CSPRNG and only its SHA-256 is stored, so a read of the table is not a set of working keys; it is checked before the form is shown, so a stale one says "expired" rather than failing after a password has been chosen; and redeeming it retires every other outstanding link. Requesting a reset cannot be used to discover who has an account — the response is byte-identical for an unknown NetID, and the send is not awaited, so a real account is not measurably slower to answer. A reset also clears the sign-in throttle, for the reason clearLoginThrottle already documented: the student resetting is usually the student the throttle locked out. Brevo over its HTTP API rather than SMTP, behind a provider-agnostic sendEmail(), so switching providers is one file; unconfigured in development it prints the link to the log instead of sending, which makes the flow testable with no API key. The professor-side reset is kept, not replaced — registration still never verifies an email address, so a student who typo'd theirs has no self-service path and still needs their professor. smoke-password-reset.ts: 21 assertions, all passing against the clone; delivery itself verified by hand, a real link clicked and redeemed.

The register

What actually stands in the way

Ordered by when it bites, not by size. The gate column is the earliest threshold at which the item stops being optional. Gate A rows have moved to the section above.

Item Gate What happens if it's skipped Size
Response uniqueness ignores the runschema.prisma:235 B @@unique([questionId, studentId]). ThemeSet was deliberately keyed by run so re-teaching starts clean; responses were not. A repeating student can't answer, or overwrites last term's data. Cheapest to migrate while the table is small. 1 day
Unbounded AI spend per account B MAX_CLASSIFY_CALLS bounds a runaway loop within one theme set; nothing bounds a term, a class, or a person. Before anyone else's lecture can spend your key, you need a cap and a kill switch. 2 days
Untested backups; no retention policy B Managed Postgres has backups. A backup you have never restored is not a backup. Student responses are education records and currently have no stated retention or deletion path — and there is at least one class of them sitting under an account nobody was tracking. 2 days
Multi-instance coordination B Detailed in the next section. Needed for zero-downtime deploys and surviving a dead instance mid-lecture — not for capacity. 1 week
SSO (Shibboleth / InCommon) C No university wants a separate password for your tool. Retires the reset flow rather than improving it — no password, nothing to reset — and brings verified identity with it. 2 weeks
LTI 1.3 / Canvas grade passback C The single largest adoption lever after "does it work". Rutgers is a Canvas school. Expect this to be the first question a department chair asks. 3 weeks
Accessibility (WCAG 2.1 AA, VPAT) C The blocker nobody anticipates. Public universities are legally required to ask. Screen-reader support, contrast, and keyboard operation on the student answer page are the minimum; a VPAT is the document they want. 3 weeks
HECVAT + a data processing agreement C HECVAT is the standard higher-ed vendor security questionnaire; you will be handed one. Holding another institution's student records also means a DPA and a FERPA position in writing. 2 weeks
A legal entity C The boundary between "my side project" and your personal assets. Required before anyone can pay you anyway. 1 week
Sizes are unhurried solo estimates including testing, not sprint points. Line references are to backend/src/ at the time of writing.

Multi-instance

Redis, but for four things — and not for the reason you'd think

The Socket.io room adapter is the part everyone thinks of and the easiest to fix: @socket.io/redis-adapter, roughly ten lines. The harder truth is that there are four pieces of process-local state, and two of them are correctness-critical rather than cosmetic.

State Where What a second instance does to it
Question clocks clock.service.tsthe clocks Map The real problem. Instance A arms a clock; instance B has no record of it, so isOpen() returns true and B keeps accepting answers after the countdown ended. The countdown silently becomes advisory — which is the one thing the whole mechanic cannot be.
Theme debounce timers themes.service.ts:529the pending Map Both instances bootstrap the same theme set concurrently: duplicate category sets on the projector, and double the model spend.
Socket.io rooms socket.ts Half the room misses question_closed and new_response. Visible, but recoverable on the next poll.
Rate limiter auth.routes.ts:30default MemoryStore The login attempt limit silently multiplies by the instance count.
Scheduler & clock sweep scheduler.ts · startClockSweep() Every instance runs both. Duplicate close emits and redundant writes; needs a leader lease, not a shared store.
The header comment in clock.service.ts already flags this: "Single-process only … if that ever changes this has to move to the database or a shared store."

The design is already shaped for it. Everything is keyed runId:questionId, so it maps onto Redis hashes with a TTL almost mechanically. The scheduler needs a SET NX lease so only one instance sweeps. Treat it as one project, not five tickets — a half-migrated clock is worse than an un-migrated one.

But be clear-eyed about why you'd do it. A 140-seat lecture is roughly three writes per second; capacity is not remotely the constraint and won't be for a long time. What multi-instance actually buys is deploying without dropping a live lecture, and surviving a crashed container mid-question. Those are real and worth having — they're just a different justification, and framing it as scale will send you optimizing the wrong things.

Unit economics

What a class actually costs to run

Worth computing precisely, because the answer is counterintuitive and it determines the entire pricing conversation. Modelled on one 140-seat course: five free-text questions per lecture, ~120 answers each, live themes and AI grading both on, 28 lectures a semester.

Call Model Rate in / out per MTok Per question Per lecture
Theme bootstrap1 call, ≤40 answers sampled Opus 5 $5 / $25 $0.032 $0.16
Theme classify~10 calls, batches of 8 Haiku 4.5 $1 / $5 $0.013 $0.07
AI grading~5 calls, batches of 25 Sonnet 4.6 $3 / $15 $0.076 $0.38
Total, everything enabled — — $0.12 $0.61
Estimated from the call shapes in the code (answer ≈ 45 tokens) at list rates, not measured. Log response.usage for a fortnight to replace these with real figures.

~$17 per class per semester in tokens. About twelve cents per student, for the whole term.

Hosting is several times that — call it $150–250 a year for the instance and Postgres — and both are rounding errors beside your time. Ten classes would cost roughly $170 a semester in model spend. A hundred classes is still under $2,000 a year.

This has one large consequence: do not build usage-based pricing. Metering tokens would cost more to build, explain, and support than the tokens themselves. The expensive resource here is you — the evening a lecture breaks, the onboarding call, the "can you export this for me" email. Price the support, not the inference.

One free improvement while you're in there

Grading still calls claude-sonnet-4-6 at $3/$15. Sonnet 5 is $2/$10 — newer and about a third cheaper on both sides. It's only about $7 a semester, so do it for the quality, not the savings. Prompt caching is not a lever at this size, incidentally: the shared prefix in the classify calls is a few hundred tokens, below the minimum cacheable prefix, so it would never hit.

Pricing

How this could be paid for

Given that marginal cost is near zero, the question isn't "what does it cost" but "what shape of money doesn't ruin the thing." Five options, roughly in order of how much of your life they consume.

Model Shape Why it works What it costs you
Open source, self-hosted Free Zero liability, zero procurement, no entity needed. People who can run it are people who won't email you at 9pm. No revenue, and in practice you still get the emails. Needs real deployment docs and the shared-invite-code assumption removed.
You eat it, departmentally Free Frictionless for the first two or three colleagues. Buys goodwill and a lot of real feedback. Fine at three classes, untenable at twenty, and there's no natural moment to start charging.
Per instructor, per semester ~$300–500 Simple to explain, scales with your actual support burden, no student-facing payment at all. Instructors rarely hold discretionary budget. Often needs a department to pay anyway — so sell it there directly.
Per student, per term ~$25–45 The incumbent model. By far the most lucrative, and the one publishers have trained the market to accept. Don't. It drags in the bookstore, inclusive-access negotiations, and refund handling — and student-pay is precisely what faculty resent about the incumbents. It undercuts your whole reason for existing.
Department or site licence ~$3–6k / yr Matches how universities genuinely buy. One invoice, one relationship, one security review, covers everyone under it. Long sales cycles, and it is the option that forces Gate C in full: HECVAT, VPAT, DPA, entity, insurance.
Ranges are directional market anchors, not quotes — confirm current incumbent pricing before repeating any figure publicly.

The recommendation: free and open for self-hosters, plus a hosted departmental licence. Keep the core clicker free forever — that is the moral position that makes the project worth talking about, and it costs you nothing real. Charge a department for the hosted instance, where the money maps honestly onto the thing that is actually scarce: your attention.

Explicitly decline per-student pricing when it comes up. Being the tool that doesn't charge students is a stronger long-term position than the extra revenue, and it's the sentence people will repeat for you.

Sequence

The order I'd actually do it in

Everything worth doing before November is done. What follows is what's left, and none of it is urgent in the way the Gate A items were.

  1. 01Run ID in the response uniqueness constraintA five-minute migration now, a data-archaeology project in two years.
  2. 02A provider behind captureException()The seam is in and every call site already routes through it, so this is an account, a DSN and one file. Deferred deliberately, not forgotten.
  3. 03Restore an actual backup to the scratch databaseA pg_dump of a live database restored cleanly, which proves the mechanism but says nothing about whether Railway's own backups are any good. That is still the untested half — and now there is somewhere to test it.
  4. 04The five integration scripts in CIA Postgres service and a booted backend in the workflow. All five pass by hand against the clone; smoke-themes.ts spends real Anthropic tokens, so it needs a decision about a CI key before it can join.
  5. 05Spend caps and a kill switchThe gate on ever letting a second person's lecture touch your API key.
  6. 06Redis: adapter, clocks, theme locks, rate limits, leader electionOne project. Do it when you want deploys that don't interrupt a lecture — not before.
  7. 07SSOEmail is in, so this is no longer what unblocks reset. It is what institutions will ask for anyway, and it retires the password entirely — along with the address-verification gap the reset flow inherited.
  8. 08LTI, accessibility, HECVAT, entityOnly once someone has actually said yes. Doing this speculatively is how side projects die.

November

What to say when somebody asks

Three versions, for the three people who will ask. The goal in each case is to be accurate and generous without accidentally promising to support them.

"Could I use this in my course next semester?"

Yes, with a caveat I'd rather say up front: right now it's built for one instructor and it hasn't been tested with two. Give me a semester of hardening and I'd genuinely like you to be the second. In the meantime I'm happy to show you the whole thing and let you watch a lecture run on it.

Why this works: it's a yes, it's honest about Gate B, and it converts an awkward ask into a specific future commitment you control the timing of.

"Could our department adopt it?"

That's a bigger conversation than the code. Departmental adoption means SSO, Canvas grade passback, an accessibility review, and a security questionnaire — none of that is exotic, but it's a few months and it doesn't make sense to build speculatively. If there's real appetite, I'd want to talk about what would fund that time.

Why this works: it names the real constraints, signals you understand institutional buying, and opens the funding conversation without naming a price on the spot.

"What does it cost to run?"

Almost nothing, and that's the interesting part. About twelve cents of model usage per student for a whole semester — an entire course runs on roughly seventeen dollars of tokens. The expensive part was never the AI, it's someone being responsible for it at 8am on a lecture day.

Why this works: it's a genuinely surprising number, it's defensible because you computed it, and it lands the point that the scarce resource is human — which is the foundation of every pricing conversation that follows.
